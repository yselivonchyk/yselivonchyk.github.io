Here we will try to put together the math of purchasing a house as a tech job employee. Not a financial advice.

# Income

There are 4 types of income we generally going to be dealing with:
- Regular income: paycheck income. Money that are coming in a paycheck every other week and taxes on which are paid immediately and in access.
- Irregular income: the bonus. Money are coming in every 12 months, taxes on this income may get underpaid.
- Equity: RSUs, ..., ..., .... An unusual form of income that is: coming in periodically every 1/3/12 months; may have weird taxation; may have limited liquidity i.e. sell only outside of lockdown window.
- 401k: withdrawn from the regular income but taxes are not paid until the liquidity takes place. Quite good, since there are multiple ways taxes can be deferred and classified.

Likely you would end up with 4 different primary financial assets:
- Hard cash. When things are turning hard cash they generally have some extra taxes to be paid.
- Investment. Your SNP500 and decaying GameStop stock. If you keep those for longer than 2y you get to pay lower tax rate (on appreciation).
- Investment: limited. Your company stock that comes with asterisks.
- Investment 401k: the untouchable.

# Withdrawal and taxes

How much money can we scrap together for an apartment?

Say, we are starting from:
- 500k in investment account in stock of all sorts
- 250k in cash (we've been selling things off at good price for a second now)
- 250k in company stock that depreciated by 50% (our employer struggles)
- 250k in untouchable 401k money.

Cash we can use right away. Mind that your overall tax rate would only be known end of the year but say we can predict it fairly quickly and we don't own uncle sam anything. So, 250k of usable money.

Investment. Out of 500k: 250k used to be 200k 2+ years ago, 150k used to be 100k 3 months ago, and 100k used to be 125k 3 months ago. 
If we were to cash out 50k would be taxable as long term investment, 25k (+50k-25k) would be taxable as regular income.
With 3.8% additional investment tax lets call it 35% and 50% effective tax rate on either.
That yields us 500k-50*.35-25*.5 = 470k.

Company stock. Lets assume taxes on these shares were paid in full when received. Average price per share at reception is 150$ and now they go for 100$. This means you previously paid taxes on 375k.
After selling you also gain another 50k of the stock of the same company and 
When cashed out (whenever they allow you do that) you gain 125k of loss.
But you also keep getting the shares since you still work there and say get another 50k worth within 30 days before and after the same.
So, these 50k carry the old tax rate you only can declare 100k of effective losses.
All in all, you get 250k of cash and another 50k of tax discount when April comes.

401k. We are not going to look into withdrawal with penalty of this money. But maybe we can borrow up to 50k and up to 50% of 401k contributions we made. Depends on the employer's plan.
Say, we invested 30k over the years, then you roll over the 401k from previous employer to have 100k+. Then you borrow 50k at 7% annual. 7% actually go back into your 401k. But be ready to repay the loan in cash the moment you are thinking of changing an employer.
So, we are lucky and we got another 50k of cash we borrow from ourselves.

That lands us 1070k of usable money. Some readily available, some coming with heavy taxes, some may have limits to execute, some are a subject for sudden repayment.

# Mortgage 101

Stock income is not always considered a regular income. Mortgage sometimes would only consider stock as regular income if you worked for the company for 24 months plus. In view of lenders it can also look like not-so-regular income and may be discounted when taken into account.

Mortgages over 720k (or 1.2M for NYC) are considered to be somewhat special since Fannie Mae can not buy it back. Probably doesn't matter much since overall folks getting such mortgages can even be desired by the banks.

Mortgage is given in such a way that you pay equal amount each months. We assume 7000$ on 1M$ (at 7.5%). Interestingly, first month you pay 7000$ about 770$ goes towards principal; at a 3.75% rate that would have been 4600$ and 1500$.

First time home buyers can discount interest payment on up to 750k of mortgage from their taxes. Which is a 52k discount with a 7% rate. It would take 15y for 1M mortgage to get under 750k in principal.

# Goldilocks

There are a couple more things to keep in mind.

Runway. After apartment purchase you end up with an unavoidable monthly bill: mortgage payment + whatever taxes, fees, insurance etc you own everybody else.
It is generally considered great to have 12-36 months of runway i.e. if you have no income whatsoever you are still able to stay liquid and not get into more debt for 12-36month.
If your monthly payment is 7000$ for the mortgage and 3000$ for the property expenses having 240k of liquid assets at the moment you buy that thing is strongly advised.

Debt-to-income ratio. So, how much of your income is going towards paying current debt. At 7000$ monthly mortgage and 20k monthly income you are at 35%, which is considered rather high.
You got to pay your taxes of 50% which leaves you with 3000$ in the pocket which conveniently goes towards the paying for the ownership of the apartment (insurance, taxes, fees).
You may still dig into the stock income to, well, eat, but what if you change the job with limited liquidity, 12m of stock income delay?

# Asset cost comparison

So, you got yourself a 2M asset. 
Underutilizing your investment and paying taxes on gains at bad time and bad rates caused you to lose 2% of net-worth
You paid 2% on top of purchasing price to enter the ownership. 
Now you are in a possession of an apartment worth 2M$.

At 10.000$ monthly expenses for mortgage and coop fees you get an apartment that would cost you 10.000$ to rent - the value gained is net even. Perhaps you could have stayed in a cheaper apartment for 3 more years though.
You can put more money into it and renovate it and enjoy it even more - the money thinking you would not have considered in a rental.
Not all cost is captured in fees and mortgage: there is also maintenance cost that may go to 500$ monthly.

1M you invested could have earned you 8% a year.
1M you borrowed is costing you 7% a year.
Property appreciates by at most 6% annually.
Your maintenance cost is about 3000$ unavoidable, another 500$ of unplanned maintenance.

First year, you pay 42k for ownership, 70k of interest, miss out on 80k of investment appreciation.
You gain 10k of equity from repaying your mortgage. Your taxes are lower by 26k due to tax discount on 52k of principle. You gain 120.000 worth of rent value.
You are losing 192-132=60k$ a year.

2010-2025 coops went up 1.8%/yr; condos 2.8%; rent 3.4%.

As time goes, the rent goes up, property value goes up. Mortgage stays flat. Coop fees go up; condo taxes stay flat.

At year 15 the same apartment goes up 50%, so does coop fee, there is still a pocketable difference of 60k compared to before. Maybe you refinanced your mortgage at 5%.
Things started to look net positive (as long as you ignore the 60k that never went anywhere 15y ago).
You are still a few years away from breaking even due to the 13-14% cost of entering and exiting the deal.
